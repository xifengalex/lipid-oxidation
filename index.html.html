Photooxidation: how light starts lipid oxidation

Light can start lipid oxidation without any help from heat or metals, but only if a sensitizer is present. The sensitizer can do this in two ways: Type I and Type II.

First, the oxygen problem

Ordinary oxygen is triplet oxygen (3O2), the ground state. It has 8 protons and 8 electrons, two of which are unpaired with parallel spins, and it is nearly inert toward unsaturated fatty acids. Singlet oxygen (1O2) is the excited state, and it is about 1500 times more reactive with unsaturated fatty acids. It can add directly to a –C=C– double bond. Making singlet oxygen needs a sensitizer such as chlorophyll or riboflavin.

Two routes, same end product

A sensitizer absorbs light and becomes excited. What it does next defines the type.

Type I sensitizer

Reacts with the fatty acid
  • The excited sensitizer reacts directly with unsaturated fatty acids.
  • Sensitizers: chlorophyll, myoglobin, riboflavin.
  • It abstracts a hydrogen, which initiates lipid oxidation.
  • Produces the same lipid hydroperoxides seen in the propagation step.
  • Radical chemistry, so it has an induction period.

Type II sensitizer

Reacts with oxygen
  • The excited sensitizer activates oxygen to singlet state.
  • Singlet oxygen then adds directly to the double bond.
  • The double bond shifts to trans.
  • No free radicals and no induction period.
  • Most common photooxidation route.

Takeaway

Both types need light and a sensitizer, and both end with lipid hydroperoxides. Type I goes straight at the fatty acid and starts a radical chain. Type II turns oxygen into the far more reactive singlet form, which reacts without radicals and without a delay.
